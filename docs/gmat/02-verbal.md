# Verbal Reasoning — 35 pitanja

20 × Critical Reasoning + 3 Reading Comprehension pasusa × 5 pitanja. Ciljno vreme: **~2 min po CR pitanju**, **6–8 min po RC pasusu** (čitanje + 5 pitanja). Rešenja su na dnu.

## Critical Reasoning

**1. (Weaken)** Last year the city installed speed cameras along Route 9. In the following twelve months, the number of accidents on Route 9 fell by 30 percent. The city council concludes that the cameras were responsible for the decline.

Which of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the council's conclusion?
(A) Surveyed drivers on Route 9 said they knew where the cameras were located.
(B) During the same period, accident rates on comparable state highways without speed cameras fell by roughly the same percentage.
(C) Operating the cameras costs less than hiring additional traffic officers.
(D) Before the cameras were installed, most accidents on Route 9 occurred at night.
(E) Some drivers slow down only when they are directly in front of a camera.

**2. (Strengthen)** Output per employee rose 12 percent in the departments of Firm V that received new ergonomic chairs. Management concludes that the chairs increased productivity.

Which of the following, if true, most strengthens management's conclusion?
(A) The chairs were more expensive than the ones they replaced.
(B) Employees who received the chairs reported that they liked them.
(C) Firm V plans to buy the same chairs for all remaining departments.
(D) The departments that received the chairs were selected at random, and output in the other departments did not change over the same period.
(E) Productivity in Firm V has risen in each of the past five years.

**3. (Assumption)** Clinical trials show that Drug Z lowers LDL cholesterol 20 percent more than Drug Y does. Because high LDL cholesterol is a major risk factor for heart attacks, patients who switch from Drug Y to Drug Z will have fewer heart attacks.

The argument depends on which of the following assumptions?
(A) Drug Z does not have other effects that raise heart-attack risk enough to offset the benefit of its greater LDL reduction.
(B) Drug Z is less expensive than Drug Y.
(C) Most patients taking Drug Y have experienced at least one heart attack.
(D) LDL cholesterol is the only risk factor for heart attacks.
(E) Patients will take Drug Z more consistently than they took Drug Y.

**4. (Boldface)** Many analysts predicted that a ride-sharing company's entry into Port City would sharply reduce taxi revenues. **Yet in the first year after its entry, taxi revenues in Port City actually rose by 6 percent.** This is not evidence that ride-sharing failed to compete with taxis, however. **Tourism in Port City grew by 25 percent that year, so taxi revenues would likely have risen far more had the ride-sharing company not entered.**

In the argument, the two portions in boldface play which of the following roles?
(A) The first is the argument's main conclusion; the second is evidence supporting that conclusion.
(B) The first is evidence supporting the analysts' prediction; the second is the argument's main conclusion.
(C) The first is an observation that appears to undermine a prediction; the second is a consideration offered to show that the observation does not in fact undermine it.
(D) The first is a prediction the argument rejects; the second is evidence against that prediction.
(E) Both are considerations the argument offers in support of the analysts' prediction, and the second is the conclusion of the argument.

**5. (Evaluate)** To reduce downtown traffic congestion, the city plans to double parking fees downtown. Officials argue that the higher fees will lead many commuters to switch from driving to public transit.

Which of the following would be most useful to know in evaluating the officials' argument?

(B) Whether the city's population is growing
(C) How much revenue the city currently collects from parking fees
(D) Whether public transit fares have changed in the last ten years
(E) What percentage of downtown commuters park in facilities whose fees are set by the city

**6. (Paradox)** Seaville had fewer rainy days last year than in any of the previous ten years, yet umbrella sales in Seaville rose by 40 percent.

### Pasus 1 (pitanja 21–25)

> For decades, just-in-time (JIT) inventory management was treated as an unambiguous triumph of operational efficiency. By synchronizing deliveries with production schedules, manufacturers slashed warehousing costs, exposed defects that large buffers had concealed, and freed capital for other uses. Toyota, whose production system popularized the approach, demonstrated that lean inventories could coexist with exceptional quality.
>
> The supply shocks of the early 2020s, however, prompted a wave of criticism. Commentators argued that JIT had made firms fragile: when a single component—most visibly semiconductors—became scarce, assembly lines halted because no buffer existed to absorb the disruption. Some predicted a wholesale shift toward "just-in-case" strategies, in which firms would hold substantial safety stocks as insurance.
>
> This narrative, though intuitive, misreads both the history and the logic of JIT. Toyota itself fared comparatively well during the chip shortage, in part because, after the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, it had mapped its supply chain several tiers deep and deliberately stockpiled a small number of components that were both critical and difficult to source. The lesson Toyota drew was not that inventory is inherently wasteful but that inventory decisions must be made item by item, weighing carrying costs against the probability and severity of disruption.
>
> Seen this way, the relevant contrast is not between lean and resilient systems but between thoughtful and mechanical applications of lean principles. Firms that treated JIT as a blanket mandate to minimize every stock level were indeed exposed; firms that understood it as a discipline for making inventory visible—and therefore manageable—were positioned to hold buffers precisely where they mattered. A wholesale retreat to just-in-case, by contrast, risks reintroducing the very inefficiencies, including hidden defects, that JIT was designed to eliminate.

### Pasus 2 (pitanja 26–30)

> Reef-building corals depend on a symbiosis with photosynthetic algae of the family Symbiodiniaceae, which live within coral tissue and supply most of the host's energy. When water temperatures rise even 1°C above the usual summer maximum for several weeks, this partnership breaks down: the algae are expelled, and the coral, stripped of its pigmented partners, turns white—a phenomenon known as bleaching. Bleached corals are not dead, but unless the symbiosis is re-established, they starve.
>
> Because symbiont types differ in heat tolerance, some researchers proposed the "adaptive bleaching hypothesis": bleaching, on this view, is not simply a failure but an opportunity for a coral to replace heat-sensitive symbionts with more tolerant ones, emerging better suited to a warmer ocean. Field observations lent the idea some support. Corals in several Indo-Pacific reefs that survived bleaching were found to host a higher proportion of the heat-tolerant genus *Durusdinium* than before.
>
> Yet the hypothesis has proven less encouraging than it first appeared. Many coral species appear capable of hosting only a narrow range of symbionts, limiting the potential for such "shuffling." Moreover, heat tolerance comes at a price: corals dominated by *Durusdinium* often grow more slowly, likely because these symbionts transfer less carbon to their hosts. And in several documented cases, corals reverted to their original symbiont communities within a few years once temperatures returned to normal, suggesting that any gain in tolerance may be temporary.
>
> None of this means that symbiont shuffling is irrelevant to reef survival. It may buy time for some populations. But researchers increasingly caution against treating it as a natural safeguard capable of offsetting the pace of ocean warming.

### Pasus 3 (pitanja 31–35)

> Economic historians long regarded medieval craft guilds as little more than cartels. By restricting entry, fixing prices, and suppressing innovation, guilds, on this view, enriched their members at the expense of consumers and retarded economic growth. Adam Smith's remark that people of the same trade seldom meet together "but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public" became a touchstone for this interpretation.
>
> Beginning in the 1990s, a revisionist school offered a more sympathetic account. In markets where buyers could not easily judge quality before purchase—cloth whose weave might loosen after a few washings, or silver of uncertain purity—guilds, the revisionists argued, solved a genuine problem. By inspecting members' output and punishing fraud, they supplied a collective reputation that no individual artisan could credibly establish. Apprenticeship requirements, similarly, could be read not as barriers to entry but as mechanisms for transmitting tacit skills that could not be written down.
>
> Critics of the revisionist account have responded that the quality-assurance functions it describes could have been, and in some places were, provided by municipal authorities without the accompanying restrictions on entry. Cities such as Antwerp in the sixteenth century flourished with comparatively weak guilds, relying instead on public inspection of export goods. If the benefits of guilds were separable from their costs, the critics contend, the persistence of guilds must be explained by their members' political power rather than by any service they rendered.
>
> The debate thus turns less on whether guilds performed useful functions than on whether those functions required guilds. That question cannot be settled by cataloguing guild regulations; it requires comparing outcomes across cities that organized quality control differently.
